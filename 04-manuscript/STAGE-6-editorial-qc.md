## Audit two: the four questions

**Would I want my mother to fill this out?**
Yes. The first forty pages are pleasure. The book earns the middle. The last thirty pages are the
reason to buy it.

**Would my mother enjoy answering these?**
Parts One through Five: yes, and she would laugh at least four times (the hair, the dumbest thing
survived, the worst boss, the lie she'd tell again). Parts Six and Seven: she would skip some, and
the book tells her that's fine on every page. Parts Eight through Twelve: these are the ones she'd
do slowly, and the ones she'd most want read.

**Would I treasure reading her answers twenty years from now?**
Every spread prompt and every marked prompt was chosen with that test. The "In my own hand" page
exists for exactly that reader.

**Does this preserve things I couldn't get from a normal biography?**
The unsaid, the almost-left, the risk not taken, the kindness nobody saw, the rule never said out
loud, what she hopes to be forgiven for, and what to do on her birthday. None of these are in any
biography, or in any competing book.

## Residual concerns, stated plainly

1. **Part Six is heavy for some writers even with permission built in.** The quick-ones page after it is deliberately gentle. If early readers report stalling there, the fix is to move Part Six's quick page to the front of the part as a warm-up. Not changed now.
2. **Part Eleven addresses "you" throughout.** A writer with several children may want to answer each prompt several times. The opener tells them to picture one person at a time; the family-question page and letters absorb the rest. Not changed now.
3. **"One last letter" has no printed prompt.** This is the right call for the book, and the riskiest single page for a nervous writer. The opener frames it. Watch it in early-reader feedback.
4. **The count of family-question pages is twelve, not thirteen.** The Letters part has no prompts and correctly has no family question. Stage 2 said thirteen; twelve is right.
