## 1. Product positioning

### The one-sentence position

A guided legacy book, written in a parent's or grandparent's own hand, that speaks directly to
the people who will one day read it.

### What it is, in the customer's words

"It's a book my mom fills out. But it's not 'tell me about your childhood.' It asks her the things
none of us ever thought to ask. And the end of it is her, talking to us."

### The category we are entering, and the one we are creating

We enter the Amazon category of guided memory journals for parents and grandparents. That category
is defined by a single sentence structure: *Tell me your story.* Every major title is written from
the child's point of view. The parent is the subject. The child is the interviewer.

We flip the address. In our book, the parent is the speaker. The title is the first line of what
they are saying. The family is the listener. This single decision does four things at once:

1. It makes the book gender-neutral without feeling generic, because "I" works for anyone.
2. It changes the emotional job from "answer my questions" to "leave your words."
3. It makes the ending inevitable. A book that starts with "what I want you to know" must end with a letter.
4. It gives us a marketing voice no competitor can use without copying us.

### Positioning statement (internal)

For adult children who want to hold onto a parent, WHAT I WANT YOU TO KNOW is the legacy book that
asks the questions no one thinks to ask, and gives a parent room to answer in their own hand.
Unlike "tell me your story" journals that walk through a timeline of favorites, it moves from
memory to honesty to a direct, personal letter to the people who will keep it.

### Emotional promise

Write it while you're here. Keep it forever.

### Emotional truth underneath (never printed as a threat)

One day your family will have questions you can no longer answer. This book lets you leave some of
the answers behind.

### Tone rules for every piece of copy

- Celebrate the life. Acknowledge the future quietly, never as a warning.
- Death is present the way it is present at a good family dinner: everyone knows, nobody makes it the topic.
- Never "before it's too late." Never "before they're gone." Never a clock.
- The word "legacy" is allowed in metadata. In the book's own voice, we say "what you leave," "your words," "keep."

## 2. Target customer

### Primary buyer: the adult child (35–60)

- Buying for a parent aged 60–85. Often a daughter buying for her mother, but sons and fathers are a real share.
- Occasion-driven: Mother's Day, Father's Day, Christmas, a 70th birthday, retirement, a health scare, a move into a smaller home.
- The trigger underneath the occasion is almost always a quiet realization: "I don't actually know that much about her."
- Has usually seen StoryWorth ads and decided against it (price, the parent won't type, or it feels like a subscription).
- Wants to look thoughtful. Fears looking morbid. The gift must be safe to hand over at a dinner table.
- Price sensitivity: low for the right object. Will pay $20–30 for something that looks like it belongs on a shelf, will not pay $20 for something that looks like a workbook.

### Secondary buyers

- Spouses, buying for each other after 30+ years of marriage.
- Grandchildren (20s–30s), often buying for a grandparent after a family event.
- Siblings pooling for a parent's milestone birthday.
- Buyers for themselves, usually 55+, who have decided to write it unprompted. (This segment grows if the book is good, because it is the one that recommends it.)

### Recipient: the writer (60–85)

- Has stories but has never written them down. Possibly hasn't written more than a card in years.
- Handwriting may be shaky. Eyesight may be tired. Both drive our specs: big page, generous rules, no small type.
- Will abandon a book that feels like homework, a form, or a test.
- Will keep going for a book that surprises them and makes them laugh on page 12.
- Does not want to be told they are dying. Does want to be told they matter.
- Needs explicit permission to skip. Needs explicit permission to be honest.

### The reader in 20 years (the person the product is actually for)

Everything in the book is designed to be read after. The handwriting, the crossed-out words, the
answers that trail off. The design must survive being reread in grief and being reread in joy.

## 3. Competitive opportunity

Full data in `competitive-research.md`. The short version:

### The market is big, proven, and beatable on quality

- The category leader (Hear Your Story series) has sold 270,000+ copies since 2018 across 21+ variants at $14.99 paperback.
- Its most pointed criticism, in its own reviews: the questions are "too dry" and "unlikely to trigger deeper recall for the truly meaningful stories."
- It wins on variant count and accumulated reviews. It does not win on prompts, design, or emotional depth.

### Six things no competitor does

1. Speaks in the writer's voice (all titles are "tell me").
2. Has a section for what was never said.
3. Has a "when you miss me" section that preserves connection without being a memorial.
4. Lets the gift-giver write a note and add questions before handing it over.
5. Ends with a letter rather than a "words of wisdom" list.
6. Looks like literary nonfiction rather than a gift-shop journal.

### Where we cannot win, and must not pretend to

- **Materials.** Premium competitors (Paige Tate, Duncan & Stone) use linen, real foil, lay-flat binding. KDP prints on standard stock with a laminated cover. We win on content, typography, and size. We do not claim "archival" anything.
- **Search terms.** "For Mom" and "for Grandma" in a competitor's title match the buyer's search exactly. Our neutral title does not. We recover this through subtitle keywords, backend keywords, A+ content, and later edition variants.
- **Review count at launch.** We start at zero against thousands. Launch plan is Stage 10's job, but the strategy must assume paid traffic and a review-seeding plan.

### The phrase we must avoid

"Questions You'll Wish You Asked" is an existing competitor's title. Our advertising concept ("there are questions you'll wish you had asked") is close enough that we should build our own language. Recommended replacement territory is in Section 7.

## 4. Recommended title and subtitle

### Title: keep it

**WHAT I WANT YOU TO KNOW**

It is the strongest thing about the concept. It is the writer speaking. It is the first sentence of
the book. It reads as a promise from the parent, not a request from the child. It is also plain
English that a 75-year-old can say out loud without embarrassment.

Risk: it is a common phrase, so Amazon search alone will not find it. Discoverability comes from the
subtitle and ads. Accept the risk. Check for title collisions in Books before finalizing (a title
cannot be owned, but a well-known book of the same name would cost us search traffic).

### Subtitle: two versions, one for the cover, one for the listing

**Cover subtitle (short, emotional, printed on the book):**

> My stories. My memories. My lessons. My words.

Slight alternative for consideration: *My stories. My memories. My words.* Three beats is cleaner
than four, and "lessons" is the weakest of the four (it leans toward advice-book). Recommend the
three-beat version.

**Listing subtitle (long, keyword-carrying, in KDP metadata and on the cover in small type if we want strict matching):**

> A Guided Legacy Journal for Parents and Grandparents to Leave Their Stories, Memories, and Words to the People They Love

Keywords carried: guided, legacy journal, parents, grandparents, stories, memories. This is the
subtitle that competes in search. It is long because the category's subtitles are long and that is
what Amazon rewards here.

KDP asks that the cover reflect the metadata title and subtitle. Recommended approach: the cover
shows the title large, the three-beat line as the display subtitle, and the long listing subtitle in
small type near the bottom of the front cover or on the spine-adjacent back. This is common practice
and passes review. If you prefer zero risk, the long subtitle becomes the only subtitle and the
three-beat line becomes a cover tagline, which KDP treats as decoration.

### Series name (needed now for extensions later)

Recommend registering a series name in KDP from day one so later editions attach to it. Options:

- **The Kept Words** (evocative, ownable)
- **Words to Keep** (plain, gift-friendly)
- **Legacy Letters** (descriptive, searchable, less ownable)

Recommend *Words to Keep*. It survives on a spine, on a Mom edition, and in an ad.

### Author name

You need a name on the cover. Options: your own name, a pen name, or an imprint name as author
(the category leader publishes under his own name plus a brand). Recommendation: a real-sounding
author name plus the series/imprint on the spine. A human name earns trust in this category.
Decision needed (Section 10).

## 5. Proposed physical specifications

### Flagship: 7 x 10 inch paperback

| Spec | Recommendation | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Trim | 7" x 10" | Big enough to write in with a normal hand. Sits on a shelf like a real book, not a workbook. |
| Interior | Black and white | Color interior roughly triples print cost with no benefit for a handwriting book. |
| Paper | Cream | Warmer, reads as "book" not "printout," kinder to older eyes under lamp light. Same price as white on KDP. |
| Page count | 240–256 pages (final count set in Stage 7) | Enough for ~200 prompts with real space plus letters and special pages. Stays under the weight where a paperback becomes floppy. |
| Cover finish | Matte | Premium feel, no glare, shows the typography. Matte laminate scuffs; the design should tolerate that (no large solid dark fields at the edges). |
| Bleed | No bleed on interior | Nothing runs to the edge in a writing book; keeps margins honest. |
| Inner margin (gutter) | 0.875" minimum | KDP minimum for 151–300 pages is 0.625". Perfect-bound books do not lay flat; extra gutter is the difference between writing comfortably and fighting the spine. |
| Outer/top/bottom margins | 0.75" / 0.75" / 0.875" | Generous, consistent with a literary interior. |
| Writing rules | Light gray (30–40% black), 9 mm spacing | Older handwriting is larger. Black rules fight the handwriting when reread; gray recedes. |
| Type | Serif for prompts, set large (12–13 pt body, prompts larger) | Nothing under 10 pt anywhere. |
| ISBN | Buy your own ISBN | Required if you ever want the book outside Amazon, and it lets the imprint name appear as publisher. |

### Cost and price (corrected at KDP setup: 7 x 10 is a large-trim size, about $1.00 + $0.017 per page for B&W)

| Pages | Print cost | List $17.99 (60%) | List $19.99 (60%) | List $21.99 (60%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 240 | $5.08 | $5.71 royalty | $6.91 | $8.11 |
| 254 (as built) | $5.32 | $5.47 | $6.67 | $7.87 |

Expanded distribution pays 40% and would net roughly $2.70–$3.50 at these prices. Ads need margin.
Recommend launching at **$19.99**, which sits above the category leader's paperback ($14.99) and at
his hardcover ($19.95). The book must look worth $5 more than his on the thumbnail. That is the
cover's job.

### Second format: KDP hardcover, 7 x 10

KDP supports 7 x 10 hardcover (case laminate, no dust jacket). Print cost is quoted by KDP at setup; expect roughly $9–10 for 254 pages at this trim;
list at $27.99–29.99. Recommend launching the hardcover at the same time
or within 30 days: gift buyers in this category trade up, and a hardcover option makes the paperback
look like the value choice. Both formats share one interior file.

### What KDP cannot do (say it plainly)

- No linen, no foil, no ribbon, no elastic, no pocket, no lay-flat binding, no dust jacket.
- Paper is standard stock. Gel pens and felt tips may show through faintly. We will recommend a pen in the "how to use this book" page.

## 6. Proposed emotional architecture

### The arc in one line

Comfortable → personal → honest → intimate → spoken directly to you.

### The four movements (13 parts)

The brief's 13-part structure is strong. One change is recommended: swap Parts Five and Six so that
*Becoming a Parent* comes before *The Hard Stuff*. Reasons:

- Parenthood is the warmest material in the book. Placing it after the career section keeps the middle of the book generous before it turns.
- *The Hard Stuff* leads naturally into *Things I Never Said*. Putting them back to back is the hinge of the whole book. Interrupting them with parenthood breaks the descent.
- A writer who has no children skips Part Five and lands in Part Six with no gap in the emotional line.

**Movement I — Memory (easy, vivid, fun)**

1. **Where I Came From** — childhood, the house, the street, grandparents, siblings, school, the first things they remember. The job of this section is to make them smile and get their pen moving.
2. **Becoming Me** — teenage years, independence, mistakes, first job, first car, first love, what they wanted to be. The job is to reveal the person before the role.

**Movement II — Life (personal, revealing)**

3. **Love & People** — friendship, love, heartbreak, marriage, the people who changed them, the people they lost.
4. **The Life I Actually Lived** — work, money, risk, travel, the turns nobody planned, what they built, what didn't work. External life: what happened.
5. **Becoming a Parent** — expectation and reality, the first meeting, fear, mistakes, pride, what parenthood taught them. Explicitly skippable.

**Movement III — Honesty (deep, private)**

6. **The Hard Stuff** — loss, fear, loneliness, decisions, starting over. Internal life: what it cost. Compassionate, optional, every prompt skippable, said in the section opener and again on the page.
7. **Things I Never Said** — the signature section. Short, sharp, devastating in the right way. Not repetitive: each prompt is a different door into the same room.
8. **What I Believe Now** — values, money, love, success, aging, happiness, what changed, what they were wrong about, what they still don't know.

**Movement IV — Legacy (intimate, spoken to you)**

9. **What I'm Proud Of** — people, choices, courage, kindness, survival, things built and changed.
10. **What I Wish I'd Done Differently** — regrets, missed chances, wrong priorities, needless worry.
11. **What I Want You to Know** — the emotional centerpiece. The writer turns and addresses the reader directly.
12. **When You Miss Me** — songs, places, food, sayings, rituals, the small things to keep doing. Connection, not memorial.
13. **Letters** — to a son, a daughter, a spouse, grandchildren, the family, the family not yet here, and one final letter.

### Structural devices that carry the arc

- **Section openers:** one right-hand page with the part number, title, and a short introduction (60–120 words) in the book's voice. The introduction says what this part is for and, where needed, gives permission.
- **Prompt pages:** one prompt per page as the default. Prompt at top, generous ruled space below.
- **Spreads:** the 10–14 most important prompts get a two-page spread.
- **"Quick ones" pages:** every part ends with one page of 6–8 short prompts (one to two lines each). These lower the bar, keep momentum, and catch the small vivid details ("the smell of your grandmother's kitchen" belongs here, not in a full page).
- **Special pages:** placed where they serve the story, never grouped. Family tree in Part One. Important people in Part Three. Favorite songs in Part Twelve. Handwriting and signature at the very end.
- **Breathing pages:** an occasional unruled page marked only "for a photograph, a drawing, or anything else." No more than one per part.

### The prompt-count plan

Target ~200 prompts, of which roughly:

- ~125 full-page prompts
- ~12 two-page spread prompts
- ~65 short prompts on "quick ones" pages

Every prompt must pass at least one of the nine tests in the brief. Stage 3 builds the inventory at
~280; Stage 4 cuts to ~200.

### Voice of the prompts

The book speaks as one person, sitting across a kitchen table, who has done this before and is not
in a hurry. Plain sentences. Occasional dry humor. Never a form. Never a therapist. Never a preacher.
No two prompts in a row share a sentence structure.

## 7. Differentiation strategy

Eight decisions that make this book feel unlike everything on the shelf.

### 1. The book is in the writer's voice

Title, section titles, and prompts all sit on the writer's side of the table. The reader is "you."
The family is "the people who will read this." Competitors ask. We speak.

### 2. "The questions nobody thinks to ask"

A named, recurring layer of prompts marked with a small typographic mark. They are the private
questions: what you were afraid of at 30, what you changed your mind about, the thing you never told
anyone, the time you almost left. This becomes a marketing asset ("including the questions no one
thinks to ask") and an in-book experience (the reader learns to look for the mark).

### 3. One flagship, gender-neutral, role-neutral

Works for Mom, Dad, Grandma, Grandpa, a stepparent, an aunt who raised you. One ASIN gathers every
review and every sale. Variant editions come later, from a position of strength, with the same
interior and a different cover and title line.

### 4. The giver is in the book

Two special pages competitors don't have:

- **"This book was given to you by..."** on the opening spread: the giver's name, the date, and three lines for why. It makes the book a two-way object and it quietly creates accountability, which is the main driver of completion.
- **"A question from your family"** at the end of each part: one blank prompt the giver writes in before gifting. The book becomes theirs before it is opened. This also solves the Mom/Dad problem: the giver personalizes it.

### 5. Room to actually write

One prompt per page. Two-page spreads for the important ones. 7 x 10. Light rules. This is the most
common complaint in the category and the cheapest one to solve. It also justifies the price.

### 6. Permission as design, not disclaimer

Permission to skip is not one page at the front. It is repeated in the openers of the hard sections,
built into prompt wording ("if you want to"), and reinforced by the "quick ones" pages that let a
tired writer still fill something in.

### 7. The ending is a letter, not a list

Competitors end with "words of wisdom" and "advice for the future." We end with a section where the
writer speaks directly to the reader, a section on how to stay close, and seven letters. The final
letter is open-ended and is the last thing in the book. Nothing after it but the signature page.

### 8. It looks like a book

Cover and interior are typographic, restrained, modern-heirloom. No florals, hearts, foil effects,
stock families, or "250 questions" badges. The thumbnail should look like literary nonfiction next to
a wall of watercolor. That contrast is the whole visual strategy.

### Marketing language territory (replacing the "questions you'll wish you asked" concept, which a competitor's title owns)

Core line: **Their story deserves to outlive them.**

Working variations for Stage 10:

- Ask while they're here.
- Some things are too important to leave unsaid.
- The things you never thought to ask. In their handwriting.
- Not "tell me your story." Their words, for you.
- A book only they can write.
- One day you'll want to hear their voice. Give them a place to leave it.
- Everything they never said, and everything they always meant to.
- You know her as Mom. This is the rest of her.

## 9. Recommendations for making it stronger

Beyond the differentiators above, these are additions and refinements worth adopting.

1. **Swap Parts Five and Six** (parenthood before the hard stuff). Argued in Section 6.
2. **Add the giver's page and the family-question pages.** They are cheap, unique, and they raise completion.
3. **Make the first prompt in the book tiny.** The first thing a nervous writer sees should be answerable in one line ("The first address you remember."). The second should be a story. The third should make them laugh. Momentum is designed, not hoped for.
4. **Add an "if you only fill out ten pages" list** to the front matter. It tells a reluctant writer where to start and quietly signals which prompts matter most.
5. **Put a "read this first" note for the future reader on the last page**, before the signature. Two lines from the book, not the writer, about what they are holding. Keep or cut in Stage 5, but draft it.
6. **Cut "lessons" from the cover line.** Three beats. "My stories. My memories. My words."
7. **Launch paperback and hardcover together** if timing allows. A single-format launch in this category leaves money on the table with gift buyers.
8. **Buy ISBNs and publish under an imprint.** It costs little and makes the series look like a publisher, not a KDP upload.
9. **Pen guidance.** A single line in "how to use this book" recommending a fine ballpoint or fine-tip archival pen. It prevents the one physical complaint we can predict.
10. **Design a "quick ones" template early** because it does more work than it looks like: it is the pressure valve for every heavy section.
11. **Name the marked prompts.** "The questions nobody thinks to ask" is a working name. It may become the strongest phrase in the marketing. Reserve it and protect it.
12. **Do not build the Mom edition until the flagship has 100 reviews.** Diluting early is the most common mistake in this category.
