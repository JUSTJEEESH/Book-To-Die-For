# Competitive Deep Dive — Every Book in the Niche, and Where Ours Beats It

Research date: September 2026. This extends `competitive-research.md` with what the leading books
actually contain: their sections, their sample prompts, their physical specs, and the documented
reasons people abandon them. Sources at the end. Amazon product pages block automated reading, so
prompt samples come from publishers' own pages, retailer listings, and third-party compilations.

The goal is not to say competitors are bad. Several are good. The goal is to be measurably better on
every dimension a buyer can see, and honest about the two where we cannot be.

---

## 1. The standard question set (what every competitor shares)

Third-party compilations of StoryWorth's prompts and the tables of contents of the print journals
converge on the same fifty or so questions. Sampled from those lists:

- What's your earliest memory? · Describe the house you grew up in. · What were your favorite foods, games, toys as a child?
- What was your mother like? What was your father like? · What did you learn from your grandparents? · What were holidays like?
- Favorite music as a teenager · Describe a memorable teacher · What did you want to be when you grew up? · What was your first car?
- How did you meet your spouse? · What was your wedding day like? · What advice would you give a young couple? · What's the secret to a long relationship?
- What was your first job? · Hardest job · A work memory you're proud of · Advice for someone starting in your field
- The day each child was born · Hardest part of parenting · What do you hope your children remember?
- Hardest period of your life · How did you get through it? · Favorite vacation · A meal you'll never forget
- Advice to your younger self · What are you most grateful for? · Most important lesson life taught you

Korie Herold's *Mom's Story* and *Grandma's Story* (the premium hardcovers) sample: "What was your
house like growing up?" "What were your favorite toys?" "What did you think you wanted to be when you
grew up?" "What was your first job?" "What family vacations do you remember most?" "What's your best
relationship advice?" "What do you value most in life?" "What's the best advice you ever received?"

Jeffrey Mason's *Hear Your Story* series (category leader): childhood, teenage years, education,
family, spirituality, work, travel, love, politics, personal reflections. 250+ questions in 110 pages
at 6 x 9.

*The Book of Myself* (a classic since 1997): 201 prompts in three life phases and five categories
(Family, Friends, Education, Work, The World).

What the compilers themselves say about these prompts: "advice" questions "produce generic
platitudes"; "What's the secret to a long relationship?" generates "greeting card quote responses";
"What advice would you give your 20-year-old self?" is "overused; most respondents deliver rehearsed
answers"; "Were you a good student?" is "a yes-or-no trap." The questions they flag as producing
"striking, quotable material" are the rare ones that go past favorites: "What's something you used to
believe that you no longer believe?" and "What do you hope people forget about you?"

**Where we stand.** Our 202 prompts cover the standard territory in roughly forty of them, and we
phrase even those to demand a scene, not a category ("What did the kitchen smell like on an ordinary
weeknight?" instead of "favorite foods"). The other 160 are outside the standard set entirely. Not
one of our prompts is "what was your favorite," "what advice would you give," or "what's the secret
to." Every competitor has all three.

## 2. The two documented reasons these books go unfinished

A memoir-services company that turned down a publisher's offer to make a prompt journal explained
why they don't work, from client experience:

1. **"Later never comes."** No one carves out time, so the book sits.
2. **The intimidation of a precious object.** "The more precious they seem, the more scary it can feel to tarnish those pretty pages."
3. **Irrelevant questions.** A 95-year-old client on "How do you relax or unwind?": "Really? I'm 95. This is not what I want to be writing about."

StoryWorth's own reviews add: recipients "answer three weekly emails and then quietly stop" because
writing is more friction than people predict. Another compilation notes the standard set has
"limited accommodation for cognitive or memory challenges" and no follow-up when a detail emerges.

**What we built in response** (some already in the book, some added in this pass):

| Failure mode | Our answer | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Later never comes | The giver's page creates a person who is quietly waiting. A new page for the giver, *If you're giving this book*, gives five specific things that get a book filled in: write why, write your own questions, ask one out loud, show them the ten-page list, don't ask when it'll be done. | Front matter, page 5 |
| Precious pages | The permission page now says it: *Cross things out. Spell things wrong. This book is not precious. What goes in it is.* The opening letter already says write in pencil and change your mind. | Front matter |
| Irrelevant questions | No lifestyle questions. Nothing about relaxing, hobbies, or favorites. Every prompt passed the nine tests. Part Five is skippable in one sentence. | Throughout |
| Writing is hard | New line in *How to use this book*: talk and let someone write it down; or record yourself and note on the page where the recording is. No competitor addresses this on the page. | Front matter |
| Where did she write about that? | A two-page index at the back, every prompt with its page number. No competitor has one. | Back matter |
| Too much at once | Quick-ones pages, the ten-page list, and start-anywhere design. | Throughout |

## 4. The two places we lose, said plainly

1. **Materials.** Herold, Chronicle, and Duncan & Stone ship lay-flat bindings, linen, foil, archival
   paper, pockets, and elastic. KDP prints a laminated paperback and a case-laminate hardcover on
   standard stock. A buyer holding both will feel the difference. Our answers: the book is bigger, the
   type is bigger, the writing space is real, and the content is in a different league. The cover is
   designed to read as premium at the thumbnail, which is where the sale happens. If the flagship
   earns it, a short-run premium edition (lay-flat, cloth) is the year-two move, sold off Amazon or
   through Amazon FBA.
2. **Reviews and years on the shelf.** Mason has eight years and hundreds of thousands of copies. That
   is a launch problem, not a product problem, and Stage 10 is built for it.

## 5. Things we deliberately did not copy

- **Question counts on the cover** ("250 questions," "100 questions"). The number is in the listing, not on the object.
- **Weekly or daily formats** (Lashier's 365-day journal; StoryWorth's 52 weeks). They create schedules, and schedules get missed.
- **Illustrations and quotes on the pages** (Good Housekeeping's book: "floral illustrations and inspirational quotes throughout"). Decoration competes with handwriting.
- **A "Spirituality" or "Religion" section.** Two prompts, asked without assumption, optional.
- **A "Politics" section** (Mason). Nothing in the book asks it.
- **Fill-in-the-blank sentences as the main form** (Levy's *Grandfather Remembers*). We use them only on finish-these pages.
- **Envelopes and sealed letters** (Redmond). The letters live in the book, next to everything else the writer said.

## 6. What changed in the book because of this research

Added in this pass, all built and in the PDF:

1. **If you're giving this book** (page 5): the giver's guide, five instructions that drive completion.
2. **The giver's page now faces the opening letter**: "This book was given to Mom by Sarah" on the left, "This book is yours" on the right.
3. **Permission page**: "Cross things out. Spell things wrong. This book is not precious. What goes in it is."
4. **How to use this book**: the dictate-or-record line, and a pointer to the index.
5. **The year I was born** (Part One): six quick prompts for era context, in the writer's memory rather than a printed almanac.
6. **My life, a line at a time** (Part Four): a one-page decade timeline the future reader will use as a map.
7. **Where to find things**: a two-page index of every prompt, special page, and letter with page numbers.

Page count moved from 250 to 254. Spine stays 0.635 in (254 x 0.0025); the cover is rebuilt to it.
