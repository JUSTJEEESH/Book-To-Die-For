## Description (HTML)

<b>There are words only you still say.</b>

The thing your mother shouted from the kitchen. The name for the soft part of the bread. The song you sang without knowing you were singing. The word that means tired, and happy, and a little sad, and evening, all at once, that has no English.

Your family has heard you say them their whole lives. They can't say them back. In a generation, nobody will.

<b>The Words We Brought With Us</b> is a keepsake for the part of a language that lived in one house. Written by the grandparent or parent who still has the words, for the children and grandchildren who want them back.

<b>Any language. Any alphabet.</b> Italian, Polish, Tagalog, Greek, Korean, Arabic, Yoruba, Vietnamese, Spanish, Portuguese, Hindi, Ukrainian, Farsi, Amharic, Punjabi: the writer supplies the language. Every label is in English; every answer is in yours.

<b>Inside:</b>
<ul>
<li>How to say my name, and your names in my language</li>
<li>Fifty-two words, two to a page: the word, how to say it, what it means, when we said it, who said it best, and a line for the one learning it to write how it sounds to them</li>
<li>Things my mother shouted from the kitchen: what she called us in trouble, what she said at the door, the blessings, the mild curses, the jokes that only work in our language, the words English doesn't have</li>
<li>Eleven songs, with the words as we actually sang them</li>
<li>The food by its real name, and a recipe in our language</li>
<li>Letters in both languages, and one to write back</li>
<li>The ten words I want you to keep</li>
</ul>

Fill it in together. Read it back badly. Be corrected. That's how the words stayed alive this long.

<b>Written by the one who still has the words. Kept by the ones who want them back.</b>

## Bullets

1. **The part of the language that lived in your house.** Not a textbook. The fifty-two words your family actually said.

7. **Letters in both languages.** Yours first. Then in English, or as close as you can get.
