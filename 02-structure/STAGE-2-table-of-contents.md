# STAGE 2 — Table of Contents, Emotional Arc, and Page Skeleton

**Status:** Awaiting approval
**Builds on:** Stage 1 (approved), `01-strategy/DECISIONS.md`

This document fixes three things: the final architecture of the book, the voice of the book
(every section introduction is written here in final-draft form), and the page budget that
Stage 7 will lay out. It also answers the owner's instruction from Stage 1: the book must work
for, and be bought for, everyone in the family.

---

## 1. One book, every relationship

The problem: competitors sell a Mom book, a Dad book, a Grandma book. A buyer with two parents and
three grandparents either buys five different books or picks one person. We want one book that
every one of those buyers picks up, and that they buy more than once.

The answer is not a neutral book. Neutral reads as generic. The answer is a book that becomes
specific in the buyer's hands and the writer's hands, without us printing five versions.

### How the interior does it

1. **The writer is "I." The reader is "you."** No prompt ever says "your children," "your husband," or
   "your grandkids" as an assumption. It says "the people you raised," "the person you built a life
   with," "if you have grandchildren." The writer supplies the nouns. A stepfather, a single mother,
   an aunt who raised you, a grandfather with no grandchildren yet: the same page works for all of them.

2. **The giver's page makes it theirs before it is opened.** Page 5 reads: *This book was given to*
   ______ *by* ______ *on* ______, with three lines for *because*. The buyer writes "Mom." The
   book is now a Mom book. Nothing on the cover had to change.

3. **"A question from your family" ends every part.** Thirteen blank prompts the giver writes in
   before wrapping it. The daughter asks her mother about the year she moved to Chicago. The
   grandson asks about the war. The book carries the family's own questions, in the family's own
   hand, next to the writer's answers. No competitor has this and no printed edition can imitate it.

4. **Letters are addressed by the writer, not by us.** Instead of "A letter to my son" and "A letter
   to my daughter" (which fails a parent of three, a parent of one, and a parent who lost one), the
   Letters section gives three letters headed *To my child* with a name line, then *To the person I
   built a life with*, *To my grandchildren*, *To my family*, *To those who come after me*, and
   *One last letter*. Every writer fills in who each letter is for.

5. **Parenthood is skippable in one sentence.** Part Five opens by telling a writer without children
   where to go next. No guilt, no gap.

6. **The book works when bought for yourself.** The opening letter has one paragraph for the person
   who bought it without being asked. That buyer is 55+, and is the one who recommends the book
   to friends.

### How the listing does it (Stage 10 will execute)

- The listing subtitle already names parents and grandparents. Backend keywords cover mom, dad,
  grandma, grandpa, nana, papa, mother, father, grandmother, grandfather, husband, wife, stepmom,
  stepdad, and the gift occasions.
- Image carousel and A+ content show the giver's page filled in five different ways: *To Mom*,
  *To Dad*, *To Grandma*, *To Grandpa*, *To my wife*. Same book, five gifts. That is the visual
  argument, and it is also the "buy more than one" argument.
- One bullet point says it plainly: one book for every parent and grandparent, so no one in the
  family is left out.
- Role editions (a *For Mom* cover line on the identical interior) come later, from the same
  series, only after the flagship has traction. They add search reach without splitting the
  product's soul.

## 2. The emotional arc

Four movements. Each part has a job, a temperature, and a prompt mix.

| Part | Title | Movement | Job | Temperature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Where I Came From | I. Memory | Get the pen moving. Make them smile. | Warm, easy |
| 2 | Becoming Me | I. Memory | Reveal the person before the role. | Warm, funny |
| 3 | Love & People | II. Life | Bring in the others. First real ache. | Personal |
| 4 | The Life I Actually Lived | II. Life | What happened, from the inside. | Personal, candid |
| 5 | Becoming a Parent | II. Life | The truth about the part nobody asks about. | Tender |
| 6 | The Hard Stuff | III. Honesty | What it cost. Fully optional. | Quiet, careful |
| 7 | Things I Never Said | III. Honesty | The hinge. Short. Sharp. | Intimate |
| 8 | What I Believe Now | III. Honesty | What survived. What changed. | Reflective |
| 9 | What I'm Proud Of | IV. Legacy | The quiet pride. | Warm again |
| 10 | What I Wish I'd Done Differently | IV. Legacy | Honest, forgiving. | Gentle |
| 11 | What I Want You to Know | IV. Legacy | Turns to face the reader. | Direct, loving |
| 12 | When You Miss Me | IV. Legacy | How to stay close. Practical, not sad. | Light, tender |
| 13 | Letters | IV. Legacy | Room. | Open |

The line to hold: the book gets deeper, not darker. Part Six is the low point of temperature, not
the end of the book. Parts Nine through Twelve climb back into warmth, and the letters are the
warmest pages in the book.

### Prompt mix per part

Every part uses at least five of the prompt forms from the brief ("Tell me about," "Do you remember,"
"Finish this sentence," "Was there ever a time," "Who was," "What changed when," and so on). No two
consecutive prompts share a form. Each part's opening prompt is the easiest one in that part. Each
part's spread prompt is its emotional center and is placed roughly two-thirds of the way through the
part, never first, never last.

The marked prompts ("the questions nobody thinks to ask") appear in every part, roughly one in five,
and increase in weight as the book progresses. In Part One the marked prompt is *What did your
parents argue about?* In Part Eleven it is *What do you hope I forgive you for?*

| 12 | Blank |
| 13 | Contents |
| 14 | Blank |

### Voice sample: the opening letter (final draft in Stage 5)

> **This book is yours.**
>
> Someone gave it to you because they want more of you than they have. Not the version they already know. The rest.
>
> There are no rules here. Skip anything. Write two words on a page, or run into the margins. Answer a question with a completely different story, because that's the one that showed up. Tape in a photograph. Draw. Write in pencil and change your mind.
>
> This is not homework. Nobody is grading it. Nobody is even waiting, not really. They're hoping.
>
> Some of these questions will be easy. Some will surprise you. A few will ask for things you've never said out loud. Those are the ones most worth answering, and the ones you are most allowed to leave blank.
>
> Write it in your own hand if you can. Your handwriting is part of what they're keeping.
>
> If you bought this book for yourself, without anyone asking, then you already know why. Somebody is going to want this someday. You're just getting there first.
>
> Start anywhere. Start today if you can.

### "How to use this book" (content, final wording in Stage 5)

- Any order. Any pace. Any pen (a fine ballpoint or fine-tip pen shows through least).
- One prompt per page. The important ones get two.
- The small mark next to some questions means: nobody usually asks this one.
- Each part ends with a page of quick ones and a page with a question from your family.
- Blank pages are for photographs, drawings, recipes, or anything the book didn't think to ask for.
- You decide who reads this, and when.

## 4. The thirteen parts, with introductions

Each introduction is the text that appears on the part's opening page, beneath the part number and
title. These are written as final drafts. Stage 6 may tighten them; the voice is set here.

### PART ONE — Where I Came From

> Start small. The house. The street. The kitchen at six in the morning. Nobody is asking for a history. They're asking what it was like to be you, before anyone called you by the name they call you now.
>
> Some of this you've told before. Tell it again, the long way. Some of it you've never said out loud. That's the part they'll read twice.

**Contents:** the first address, the house, the kitchen, the street and neighbors, parents as people, what the parents argued about (marked), grandparents, siblings, school, the friend from then, the family's food, holidays as they actually were, church or its absence handled without assumption, the first thing they remember wanting, a family story everyone tells and the true version (spread).
**Special page:** Family tree (two pages, hand-drawn style, three generations up and two down, room for names and dates, nothing required).
**Ends with:** Quick ones. A question from your family.

### PART TWO — Becoming Me

> Somewhere between the child and the adult, there was a person your family has never met. The one who lied about where they were going. The one with a plan. The one who was sure about everything.
>
> This section is for that person. Be honest about them. They turned out fine.

**Contents:** the bedroom, the music, the first job and the first paycheck, the first car or the lack of one, first love and what it actually was, the friend group, the thing they got in trouble for (marked), what they wanted to be, the teacher or adult who saw them, the day they felt like an adult, leaving home, the thing they believed at nineteen that they'd laugh at now, a mistake from that age that turned out to matter (spread).
**Ends with:** Quick ones. A question from your family.

### PART THREE — Love & People

> Nobody becomes who they are alone. This is where the other people come in. The ones who stayed. The ones who didn't. The person you married, or almost did. The friend whose number you still know by heart.
>
> Some of these pages will be easy. One or two won't. Take them in any order.

**Contents:** the best friend, a friendship that ended and why, the first heartbreak, how they met the person they built a life with, what they knew on the wedding day and what they didn't, what a long relationship is actually made of, the fight they still remember, someone who changed the direction of their life, someone they lost, what they'd say to that person now (marked), the person they never got to thank, the kind of love they didn't expect (spread).
**Special page:** The important people (two pages: name, who they were to you, one line each, twelve rows).
**Ends with:** Quick ones. A question from your family.

### PART FOUR — The Life I Actually Lived

> There's the life you planned, and there's this one. Work, money, the moves, the risks, the long stretches where nothing seemed to happen and the single afternoons that changed everything.
>
> Your family knows the outline. They don't know what it felt like from the inside. That's what these pages are for.

**Contents:** the work they actually did versus what it says on paper, the best day at work, the worst boss, money and what it meant in their house, the poorest year, the risk they took, the risk they didn't (marked), a place they went that changed them, the move, the thing they built, the thing that failed, the turn nobody planned, what they'd tell someone starting the same road (spread).
**Ends with:** Quick ones. A question from your family.

### PART FIVE — Becoming a Parent

> If you never had children, turn to Part Six. Nothing here is required, and nothing is missing without it.
>
> If you did: nobody tells the truth about this part, mostly because nobody asks. What you expected. What you got. The things you'd do again in a second, and the things you still think about at night. Your children may read this. Tell them anyway.

**Contents:** finding out, the first time they held or met their child, what they were afraid of, what they were wrong about, the hardest year, the thing their child did that undid them, a mistake they'd take back (marked), what they were secretly proud of, what they never told their child they noticed, what parenthood took and what it gave, the day they realized their child was a person (spread).
**Ends with:** Quick ones. A question from your family.

### PART SIX — The Hard Stuff

> You can skip this whole section. That isn't a formality. It's the rule.
>
> Every life has years it doesn't talk about. You don't owe anyone those years. But if there's something you've carried a long time, and you've ever wished someone knew, this is a place to set it down. Write as much or as little as you want. Stop whenever you like. The book will still be here tomorrow.

**Contents:** a loss, what grief was actually like, the loneliest stretch, a decision with no good option, a time they almost gave up (marked), what they were most afraid of and whether it happened, starting over, what they were ashamed of and no longer are, who showed up, what they learned that they'd rather not have learned, what got them through (spread).
**Every prompt page in this part carries a small line at the bottom:** *Skip this one if you want. Nothing is lost.*
**Ends with:** Quick ones (gentle: "a song that got me through," "a person who called at the right time"). A question from your family.

### PART SEVEN — Things I Never Said

> This is the shortest section in the book, and the one most people will read first when it's their turn.
>
> Everyone has sentences they never finished. Some are apologies. Some are thank-yous. Some are just the truth, waiting for a better moment that never came. Here is the moment. You don't have to say everything. Say one thing.

**Contents (each a different door, never the same shape twice):** something I've wanted to tell you for years; something I was afraid would change how you saw me; something I should have apologized for, and didn't; something I misunderstood about you for a long time; something I never told anyone (marked); something I understand now that I didn't then; something I said once that I wish I could take back; something I never said out loud because I thought you already knew (spread).
**Ends with:** *Finish these sentences* (replaces quick ones here: "I never told you that ___," "I was wrong about ___," "I always meant to ___"). A question from your family.

### PART EIGHT — What I Believe Now

> Not what you were taught. Not what you used to think. What you actually believe, now, after all of it. About money, about love, about what matters and what turned out not to.
>
> You're allowed to have changed your mind. You're allowed to still not know. Both are worth writing down.

**Contents:** what they believe about love, money, work, luck, God or the absence of one (asked without assumption), what makes a good life, what they were completely wrong about (marked), what they thought would matter and didn't, what aging is actually like, what they still don't know, what they'd defend to anyone, the rule they live by that they've never said out loud (spread).
**Ends with:** *What I believe, in one line each* (quick ones variant). A question from your family.

### PART NINE — What I'm Proud Of

> Most people are bad at this one. They reach for the obvious thing, then feel awkward and stop.
>
> Go past the obvious thing. The pride that counts is usually quieter: something you didn't do, someone you stood by, a day you didn't quit. Nobody is going to accuse you of bragging. This is your book.

**Contents:** a thing they built, a person they stood by, a day they didn't quit, something they changed about themselves, a kindness nobody saw (marked), the thing they survived, what they did right as a parent or with the people they raised, a moment of courage that looked small, the thing they'd want on the record (spread).
**Ends with:** Quick ones. A question from your family.

### PART TEN — What I Wish I'd Done Differently

> Every honest life has a few of these. Writing them down isn't confession and it isn't punishment. It's the most useful thing you can hand to someone younger: here is where I went wrong, and here is what I'd tell you instead.
>
> Be kind to the person who made those choices. They didn't know what you know now.

**Contents:** a chance they didn't take, a person they didn't appreciate in time, the thing they worried about for years that never happened, what they'd have spent less time on, what they'd have spent more time on, a priority they got backwards (marked), an apology they still owe, what they'd tell themselves at thirty, what they've forgiven themselves for and what they haven't (spread).
**Ends with:** Quick ones. A question from your family.

### PART ELEVEN — What I Want You to Know

> Everything before this was the story. This is the part where you turn and look at the people reading it.
>
> Not everyone at once. Picture one of them. Write to that one. Then, if you want, picture the next. These are the pages they'll come back to.

**Contents:** what I see in you that you don't see, what I'm proud of you for that has nothing to do with achievement, what I hope you never change, what I hope you understand about the choices I made, what I want you to know about me that has nothing to do with being your parent or grandparent (spread 1), what I hope you forgive me for (marked), what I hope you remember about this family, what to do when life gets hard, what I want you to stop worrying about, the thing I'd say if I only had a minute (spread 2).
**Special page:** Family sayings (things we always said, and what they actually meant; ten rows).
**Ends with:** A question from your family. (No quick ones here. The part should end on the family's question.)

### PART TWELVE — When You Miss Me

> Someday the people who love you will want to feel close to you and you won't be in the next room. This isn't a sad section. It's a practical one.
>
> Tell them where to find you. The song, the road, the recipe, the thing you always said. Give them something to do with their hands. Give them a reason to laugh at exactly the moment they need one.

**Contents:** the song to play, the place to go, the thing to cook, the smell that will always be me, the thing we did together that you should keep doing, the joke to tell, what to do on my birthday, what I hope you hear in your head when you need it, what I don't want you to do (marked; gently: "don't keep the house like a museum"), the small thing of mine to keep, a tradition to start after I'm gone (spread).
**Special pages:** Favorite songs (one page: title, who sang it, why). The recipe (two pages: one recipe, in your handwriting, with the part that isn't written down).
**Ends with:** Quick ones (the lightest in the book: "my coffee order," "the word I always mispronounced"). A question from your family.

### PART THIRTEEN — Letters

> There are no prompts here. Just the names of the people you love, and room.
>
> Write one letter or all of them. Write them badly. Cross things out. A letter in your handwriting, with your mistakes in it, will be worth more to them than anything perfect.
>
> The last letter is for whoever needs it, whenever they need it. Take your time with that one.

**Letters (each with a "To ______" line):**
1. To my child (two pages)
2. To my child (two pages)
3. To my child (two pages)
4. To the person I built a life with (two pages)
5. To my grandchildren (two pages)
6. To my family (two pages)
7. To those who come after me (two pages)
8. **One last letter** (four pages). The heading is the only prompt: *If you're reading this and I'm not there to answer you...* is NOT printed. The page reads simply: *One last letter.* The opener above has already framed it. The writer supplies the sentence.
